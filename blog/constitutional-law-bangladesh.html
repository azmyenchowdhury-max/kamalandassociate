Constitutional Law in Bangladesh: Rights, Remedies & Writ Jurisdiction

Adv. Mustafa Kamal Chowdhury
Senior Advocate, High Court Division
February 1, 2026 10 min read

Primary Keyword: Constitutional Law in Bangladesh

Secondary Keywords: fundamental rights Bangladesh • writ petition Bangladesh • judicial review Bangladesh • Article 102 Bangladesh • habeas corpus Bangladesh • public interest litigation Bangladesh

Key Legal Points
  • The Constitution of Bangladesh (1972) is the supreme law — any law inconsistent with it is void to the extent of the inconsistency.
  • Part III of the Constitution (Articles 26–47A) guarantees fundamental rights enforceable directly through the courts.
  • Article 102 empowers the High Court Division to issue writs of certiorari, mandamus, prohibition, habeas corpus, and quo warranto.
  • The Supreme Court of Bangladesh — comprising the High Court Division and the Appellate Division — is the ultimate guardian of the Constitution.
  • Constitutional amendments require a two-thirds majority in Parliament, and core provisions cannot be altered in ways that destroy the basic structure of the Constitution.

Introduction

Constitutional law in Bangladesh forms the bedrock of the entire legal system. It defines the relationship between the state and the individual, distributes power among the executive, legislature, and judiciary, and — most importantly for individual citizens and businesses — guarantees a set of fundamental rights that are directly enforceable through the courts.

For individuals facing arbitrary state action, businesses challenging unlawful administrative decisions, and anyone whose fundamental rights have been violated, the Constitution provides one of the most powerful legal remedies available in Bangladesh: the writ jurisdiction of the High Court Division under Article 102. Understanding when and how this remedy is available — and how it interacts with the ordinary court system — is essential to protecting your legal rights effectively.

This article explains the constitutional framework in Bangladesh — covering fundamental rights, the writ jurisdiction, judicial review of administrative action, and how Kamal & Associates assists clients in enforcing their constitutional rights before the Supreme Court.

Legal Framework in Bangladesh

1. The Constitution as Supreme Law

Article 7 of the Constitution of Bangladesh declares the Constitution to be the supreme law of the Republic, and provides that any other law inconsistent with the Constitution is void to the extent of the inconsistency. This supremacy is what gives constitutional rights their practical force — a citizen whose rights are violated by an ordinary statute or an administrative decision can challenge that statute or decision as unconstitutional, and the courts have the power to strike it down or restrain its enforcement.

2. Fundamental Rights (Part III)

Part III of the Constitution, comprising Articles 26 to 47A, guarantees a comprehensive set of fundamental rights, including equality before the law (Article 27), non-discrimination (Article 28), the right to life and personal liberty (Article 32), protection against arbitrary arrest and detention (Article 33), freedom of movement, assembly, association, thought, conscience, and speech (Articles 36–39), and the right to property (Article 42). Article 26 specifically provides that any existing law inconsistent with these fundamental rights shall, to the extent of the inconsistency, become void, and that the state shall not make any law inconsistent with these rights.

3. Article 102 — Writ Jurisdiction

Article 102 of the Constitution empowers the High Court Division of the Supreme Court to issue five types of writ, mirroring the traditional prerogative writs of English common law: certiorari (to quash an unlawful decision of a lower court, tribunal, or public authority), mandamus (to compel a public authority to perform a legal duty it has failed to perform), prohibition (to prevent a lower court or authority from exceeding its jurisdiction), habeas corpus (to secure the release of a person unlawfully detained), and quo warranto (to challenge a person's authority to hold a public office). This writ jurisdiction is one of the most direct and powerful tools available to individuals and businesses seeking to challenge unlawful government or administrative action.

4. Standing and Public Interest Litigation

While the traditional requirement for bringing a writ petition is that the petitioner is a "person aggrieved" with a direct personal interest in the matter, the High Court Division has, over recent decades, developed a body of public interest litigation (PIL) jurisprudence that allows individuals and organisations to bring writ petitions on behalf of the broader public interest in appropriate cases — particularly concerning fundamental rights violations affecting a class of people who may not themselves be in a position to approach the court.

5. Constitutional Amendment and the Basic Structure Doctrine

Article 142 permits Parliament to amend the Constitution by a two-thirds majority. However, the Appellate Division has recognised — most notably in the landmark Anwar Hossain Chowdhury case — that certain core or "basic structure" features of the Constitution, including its fundamental rights framework and the independence of the judiciary, cannot be amended in a manner that destroys the essential character of the Constitution itself. This doctrine places an important substantive limit on the constitutional amendment power.

Common Legal Issues

1. Unlawful Arrest and Detention

Where a person is arrested or detained without lawful authority — including detention beyond permissible periods without production before a magistrate, or detention under preventive detention laws applied without following required procedural safeguards — a habeas corpus petition under Article 102 can secure their immediate release. Given the urgency inherent in unlawful detention cases, these petitions require immediate legal action.

2. Unlawful Administrative Decisions

Government agencies and public authorities in Bangladesh frequently make decisions — refusing licenses, terminating employment, cancelling permits, or imposing penalties — that exceed their legal authority, fail to follow required procedure, or are otherwise unlawful. A writ of certiorari can quash such decisions, while mandamus can compel an authority to perform a duty it has unlawfully refused to perform, such as processing an application it is legally obliged to process.

3. Discriminatory or Arbitrary State Action

Where a law or administrative action treats individuals or businesses unequally without reasonable justification — in violation of Articles 27 and 28 — affected parties can challenge the law or action as unconstitutional. This is a particularly important remedy for businesses facing arbitrarily inconsistent regulatory treatment compared to competitors.

4. Challenges to Public Appointments

Where a person holds or claims to hold a public office without proper legal authority — for example, due to a defective appointment process or disqualification — a writ of quo warranto can be used to challenge their right to hold that office. These proceedings are often significant in disputes over corporate regulatory bodies, statutory authorities, and other public institutions.

5. Delay and Procedural Barriers in Writ Petitions

While the writ jurisdiction is a powerful remedy, it is subject to important procedural constraints — including the general requirement to exhaust alternative statutory remedies where an adequate alternative remedy exists, and the discretionary nature of the writ jurisdiction itself. Petitioners frequently face delay, procedural objections, and the risk that alternative remedies will be found to bar the writ petition. Skilled procedural strategy from the outset significantly improves the prospects of a writ petition being entertained and heard promptly.

Practical Tips for Clients

  • Act immediately in detention cases: Habeas corpus and other urgent constitutional remedies are time-critical. Contact specialist counsel the moment you become aware of an unlawful arrest or detention — delay can materially affect both the legal and practical outcome.
  • Exhaust or address alternative remedies strategically: Because the writ jurisdiction is generally not available where an adequate alternative statutory remedy exists, seek legal advice early on the correct procedural route — pursuing the wrong remedy first can waste critical time.
  • Document administrative decisions carefully: If you are challenging a government or regulatory decision, obtain and preserve all relevant correspondence, orders, and procedural records — these form the essential evidentiary basis for a successful writ petition.
  • Understand that writ relief is discretionary: Even where the legal grounds exist, the High Court Division retains discretion over whether to grant writ relief. A well-prepared petition that clearly demonstrates urgency, merit, and the absence of alternative remedies significantly improves the prospects of relief being granted.
  • Consider the wider implications: Constitutional challenges to legislation or major regulatory schemes can have implications well beyond an individual case. Seek specialist advice on the strategic and practical consequences of bringing a constitutional challenge, not just its legal merits.

FAQs

1. What is a writ petition and when can I file one?
A writ petition is an application to the High Court Division of the Supreme Court under Article 102 of the Constitution, seeking one of five types of relief: certiorari, mandamus, prohibition, habeas corpus, or quo warranto. You can generally file a writ petition when a government body, public authority, or lower court or tribunal has acted unlawfully, exceeded its jurisdiction, failed to perform a legal duty, or unlawfully detained a person — provided there is no adequate alternative statutory remedy available, or the case otherwise falls within the scope of the writ jurisdiction. Specialist legal advice is essential to determine whether a writ petition is the correct and available remedy in your specific circumstances.

2. How quickly can a habeas corpus petition be heard?
Habeas corpus petitions, given their subject matter — the unlawful deprivation of a person's liberty — are treated by the High Court Division as urgent matters and can often be heard and disposed of considerably faster than ordinary civil or writ proceedings. The exact timeline depends on the circumstances of the case, but immediate legal action, including prompt filing supported by clear evidence of the unlawful detention, is critical to securing the fastest possible relief.

3. Can I challenge a law as unconstitutional?
Yes. Under Article 26 of the Constitution, any existing law inconsistent with the fundamental rights guaranteed in Part III is void to the extent of the inconsistency, and the High Court Division has the power, through the writ jurisdiction, to declare such a law unconstitutional and unenforceable. Challenging legislation on constitutional grounds is a significant undertaking that requires careful legal analysis of both the challenged provision and its practical impact, and should be pursued with specialist constitutional law advice.

4. What is the difference between an ordinary appeal and a writ petition?
An ordinary appeal challenges a decision on its merits within the existing statutory appeal framework, typically before a designated appellate body or court. A writ petition, by contrast, is a constitutional remedy that challenges the lawfulness of a decision or action itself — including whether the decision-maker had the legal authority to act, followed required procedure, or acted rationally — and is generally available where no adequate alternative remedy, such as a statutory appeal, exists. Choosing the correct route between an appeal and a writ petition is a critical strategic decision that should be made with specialist legal advice.

5. Can a business use the writ jurisdiction to challenge a regulatory decision?
Yes. Businesses frequently use writ petitions to challenge unlawful decisions by regulators and government agencies — including licence refusals, permit cancellations, and discriminatory regulatory treatment — where the decision exceeds the authority's legal powers, fails to follow required procedure, or is otherwise unlawful or irrational. As with individual writ petitions, businesses must generally show that no adequate alternative statutory remedy exists, and should seek specialist legal advice promptly once an adverse regulatory decision is made, given applicable time limits and procedural considerations.
