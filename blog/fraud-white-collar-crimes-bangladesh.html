Fraud and White Collar Crimes in Bangladesh: Legal Framework, Offences & Defence Strategy

Adv. Mohammad Mostofa Kamal
Head of The Chamber & Founder, Kamal & Associates
February 8, 2026 11 min read
Key Legal Points
  • White collar crimes in Bangladesh are prosecuted under multiple overlapping statutes — principally the Penal Code, 1860 (cheating under Sections 415–420, forgery under Sections 463–471, and criminal breach of trust under Section 406), alongside specialised legislation.
  • The Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC), established under the Anti-Corruption Commission Act, 2004, investigates and prosecutes corruption and financial crimes involving public officials, banking fraud, and large-scale financial irregularities.
  • Money laundering under the Money Laundering Prevention Act, 2012 is broadly defined to cover the concealment, transfer, or use of proceeds derived from any of a long list of predicate offences, and carries severe criminal penalties.
  • Accused persons in white collar crime investigations in Bangladesh have constitutionally protected rights — including the right against self-incrimination, the right to legal representation, and the right to bail in appropriate circumstances.
  • White collar crime defendants frequently face parallel criminal and civil exposure — including cheque dishonour claims under the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, which is one of the most heavily litigated financial offences in Bangladesh.

Introduction

Fraud and white collar crimes represent some of the most serious and complex legal challenges faced by businesses, executives, financial professionals, and individuals in Bangladesh. Unlike conventional crime, white collar offences typically involve intricate financial transactions, corporate structures, and documentary evidence — requiring specialist legal expertise to investigate, prosecute, or defend effectively.

As Bangladesh's financial sector and corporate economy have grown, so too has the sophistication and scale of financial crime — from cheque fraud and loan default schemes through to complex money laundering and corporate embezzlement cases. In parallel, enforcement bodies including the Anti-Corruption Commission, Bangladesh Financial Intelligence Unit, and Bangladesh Bank have significantly increased their investigative and prosecutorial activity.

This article explains the legal framework governing fraud and white collar crime in Bangladesh — covering the key offences, investigative bodies, common legal issues facing both victims and accused persons, and how Kamal & Associates provides specialist criminal defence and complainant representation in white collar crime matters.

Legal Framework in Bangladesh

1. The Penal Code, 1860

The Penal Code, 1860 remains the foundational criminal statute for most fraud-related offences in Bangladesh. Cheating (Sections 415–420) covers dishonestly inducing a person to deliver property or do or omit to do something through deception, and carries enhanced penalties where the offence involves property or a promise to deliver property. Criminal breach of trust (Section 406) addresses the dishonest misappropriation of property entrusted to a person, and is frequently charged in cases involving company directors, agents, and employees who misuse funds or assets placed in their care. Forgery (Sections 463–471) covers the making of a false document with intent to cause damage, deceive, or commit fraud, including forged financial instruments and corporate documents.

2. The Anti-Corruption Commission Act, 2004

The Anti-Corruption Commission (ACC) is a constitutionally recognised independent body with broad powers to investigate and prosecute corruption offences, including bribery, abuse of public office, illegal accumulation of wealth, and — significantly for the private sector — banking fraud and large-scale financial irregularities involving public funds or public institutions. The ACC has extensive investigative powers, including the ability to summon witnesses, seize documents, and freeze assets, and its involvement in a matter significantly raises the stakes for any individual or business under investigation.

3. The Money Laundering Prevention Act, 2012

The Money Laundering Prevention Act, 2012 criminalises money laundering — broadly defined to include the conversion, transfer, concealment, or acquisition of property known to be the proceeds of crime, as well as attempting or assisting in such activity. The Act operates alongside a wide range of predicate offences (crimes whose proceeds, if laundered, trigger money laundering liability), meaning that individuals and businesses under investigation for underlying fraud or corruption offences frequently face parallel money laundering charges, which carry their own severe penalties including substantial fines and imprisonment.

4. The Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881

Cheque dishonour — where a cheque is returned unpaid due to insufficient funds or other reasons — is a criminal offence under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, and is one of the most commonly litigated financial matters in Bangladesh's courts. While technically a civil payment default, the criminal cheque dishonour process gives payees a powerful enforcement tool, and defendants in these cases face genuine criminal exposure, including imprisonment, alongside the underlying civil debt.

5. Corporate and Regulatory Fraud Provisions

Beyond the general criminal law, sector-specific regulatory frameworks — including Bangladesh Bank regulations governing the banking sector, Bangladesh Securities and Exchange Commission (BSEC) regulations governing capital markets, and the Companies Act, 1994 governing corporate conduct — create additional layers of liability for financial misconduct, including regulatory sanctions that can apply alongside or independently of criminal prosecution.

Common Legal Issues

1. ACC Investigations and Summons

Individuals or businesses summoned by the Anti-Corruption Commission face a formal, high-stakes investigative process. How a person responds to an ACC summons — including whether and how they engage with investigators, what documents they provide, and whether legal counsel is present — can significantly affect the eventual outcome of the matter. Given the ACC's broad powers and the seriousness of corruption allegations, immediate specialist legal representation upon receiving an ACC summons is essential.

2. Cheque Dishonour Cases

Businesses and individuals frequently find themselves as either complainant or accused in cheque dishonour proceedings under the Negotiable Instruments Act. For complainants, correctly following the statutory notice and filing procedure is essential to preserving the right to prosecute. For accused persons, a range of defences may be available depending on the circumstances of the cheque issuance and dishonour, and early legal advice is important given the criminal exposure involved.

3. Corporate Embezzlement and Employee Fraud

Businesses that discover employee or director misappropriation of funds face difficult decisions about whether and how to pursue criminal complaints, civil recovery action, or both, while managing the practical and reputational implications of the discovery. A well-coordinated legal strategy — combining criminal complaint, civil asset recovery, and, where relevant, employment law action — typically produces better outcomes than an uncoordinated response.

4. Money Laundering Exposure in Ordinary Commercial Disputes

Because money laundering liability attaches to the proceeds of a wide range of predicate offences, individuals and businesses involved in what may appear to be an ordinary commercial or financial dispute can find themselves facing money laundering allegations as well — significantly raising the legal stakes. Recognising this risk early, and structuring both the defence and any settlement discussions with this exposure in mind, is an important part of effective legal strategy in financial disputes.

5. Bail and Pre-Trial Detention

White collar crime allegations, particularly those involving the ACC or large sums of money, can result in arrest and pre-trial detention. Securing bail promptly — and understanding the factors the courts consider in white collar crime bail applications, which can differ from ordinary criminal matters — is often the most immediate and urgent legal priority for an accused person, and requires experienced criminal defence representation from the earliest stage of a case.

Practical Tips for Clients

  • Never respond to an ACC or police summons without legal advice: What you say and provide in an initial investigative interview can have a lasting effect on your case. Obtain specialist legal representation before engaging substantively with any investigative body.
  • Preserve documents immediately: Whether you are a potential complainant or a person concerned that allegations may be made against you, preserve all relevant financial records, correspondence, and documentation immediately — do not wait until a formal investigation begins.
  • Act quickly on cheque dishonour matters: The Negotiable Instruments Act imposes strict statutory time limits for notice and filing. If you have received a dishonoured cheque, or been notified of one, seek legal advice immediately to avoid losing your rights on a technicality.
  • Coordinate criminal and civil strategy: If you are a victim of fraud or embezzlement, decide your criminal complaint and civil recovery strategy together, with specialist legal advice — pursuing them separately and without coordination often produces worse outcomes for both.
  • Understand your bail rights: If you or a family member has been arrested in connection with a white collar crime allegation, immediate legal representation focused on securing bail is typically the most urgent priority, alongside building the substantive defence.
Practical Insight

White collar crime enforcement in Bangladesh — particularly through the ACC and increasingly assertive banking sector regulators — has intensified considerably in recent years, and the documentary, financial nature of these cases means that early legal strategy matters more, not less, than in conventional criminal matters. The single most consequential decision a person or business under investigation makes is often the very first one: how they respond to the initial contact from an investigating authority. Clients who obtain specialist legal representation from the outset — before any statement is given or document produced — consistently achieve better outcomes than those who engage counsel only after the investigation has progressed. FAQs

1. What should I do if I am summoned by the Anti-Corruption Commission?
You should obtain specialist criminal defence legal representation immediately, before responding substantively to the summons or providing any statement or documents. The ACC has broad investigative powers, and how you engage with an ACC investigation from the very first contact can significantly affect the outcome of the matter. Do not attempt to handle an ACC summons without legal representation, and do not delay in securing counsel once you become aware of an investigation.

2. Can I go to jail for a bounced cheque in Bangladesh?
Yes. Cheque dishonour is a criminal offence under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881, and conviction can result in imprisonment as well as financial penalties, in addition to the underlying civil debt obligation. If you have issued a cheque that has been dishonoured, or you are facing a cheque dishonour complaint, you should seek legal advice immediately — there are statutory notice periods and available defences that must be addressed promptly and correctly.

3. What is the difference between fraud and money laundering charges in Bangladesh?
Fraud offences — such as cheating, criminal breach of trust, and forgery under the Penal Code — concern the underlying dishonest act itself. Money laundering, under the Money Laundering Prevention Act, 2012, concerns the subsequent concealment, transfer, or use of the proceeds of that underlying offence (or various other predicate offences). It is common for a person facing a fraud allegation to also face parallel money laundering charges if the proceeds of the alleged fraud were moved, concealed, or used in a way that falls within the Act's broad definition — meaning the potential penalties and legal complexity of a case can be significantly greater than the underlying fraud allegation alone suggests.

4. My company has discovered employee embezzlement — what are my options?
You generally have both criminal and civil options, which are not mutually exclusive: filing a criminal complaint (typically for criminal breach of trust or cheating) to pursue prosecution of the responsible individual, and pursuing civil recovery action to recoup the misappropriated funds or assets. In many cases, a coordinated strategy pursuing both routes simultaneously — supported by a thorough internal investigation and careful evidence preservation from the moment the embezzlement is discovered — produces the best outcome. Specialist legal advice should be sought as soon as embezzlement is suspected, both to preserve your legal options and to manage the practical and reputational implications appropriately.

5. Can I get bail if I am arrested in a white collar crime case?
Bail is available in most white collar crime cases in Bangladesh, though the likelihood and conditions depend on the nature and severity of the allegations, the evidence against the accused, and — particularly in ACC matters involving large sums — factors specific to that investigation. Securing bail promptly is typically an urgent priority following any arrest, and should be pursued by experienced criminal defence counsel immediately, in parallel with building the substantive defence to the underlying allegations.
